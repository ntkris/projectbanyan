# Content plan and first push

A working paper for the founding meeting. Three parts: what the content is,
how we verify it, and how people find it. Decisions we need to make are at
the end.

The short version: our first product is a small library of plain language
answers, and every page carries the name of a doctor and the name of a
person living with Parkinson's who both signed it off. Small and trusted
beats big and generic.

---

## 1. What the content actually is

### The trap to avoid

Parkinson's UK, the Michael J. Fox Foundation, Davis Phinney and Mayo Clinic
all have large libraries already. We will not out-publish them and we should
not try. If we write a general "What is Parkinson's" page, we have built the
fifth best version of a page that already exists.

So the question is not "what topics". It is "what do we do that the big
sites do not".

### Three things we can do that they do not

**We can be readable.** Every rule on the site is built for eyes and hands
affected by Parkinson's. 19px type, 7:1 contrast, short lines, no jargon.
Most Parkinson's information is written for people who read easily. Ours is
not. That is a real claim and we should say it out loud.

**We can organise around moments, not medicine.** Other sites file content
by topic. Symptoms, treatments, therapies. People do not arrive with a
topic. They arrive with a moment. "They said it might be Parkinson's and the
scan is in three months." "Dad fell again last night." "I have ten minutes
with the neurologist on Thursday." Content built for the moment gets used.
Content built for the taxonomy gets skimmed.

**We can be specific about where the reader lives.** Almost all good
Parkinson's content assumes the NHS or US insurance. If we anchor in India,
very little exists. Local drug names and costs, how to find a movement
disorder specialist, what a general neurologist will and will not do, how
family caregiving actually works here. That is a real gap, and our network
sits inside it. This is decision two at the end.

### Four kinds of thing we would publish

These do different jobs, so treat them differently.

**Answer pages.** The core unit. One question, one page, 400 to 700 words.
The title is the question a real person asks. "What happens at your first
neurology appointment." "Why do my medicines stop working before the next
dose." "Is it safe to keep driving." Cheap to make, easy to review, easy to
keep current, and this is where search traffic comes from. Target 20 to 30
in the first year, not 200.

**Guides.** Short numbered sequences that walk someone through a period. Two
to start. "The first 90 days after a diagnosis" for the person, and the same
for the carer. Each is five to seven answer pages in a fixed order, with a
clear beginning and end.

**Tools you can print.** The highest value tier, and the thing that
actually spreads.

- A one page symptom and medication diary that a doctor finds useful.
- "Ten questions to ask at your appointment", with room to write answers.
- A medication timing chart for the fridge.
- A one page explainer a carer can hand to relatives.

Doctors give these out. Support groups photocopy them. Every one carries our
address. This is our best distribution asset and it is genuinely useful.

**Voices.** Real accounts from real people. The poem already on the site
does more than any explainer could. A short interview, a paragraph, one
recorded answer. Nobody else can copy this, and it is what makes the site
feel like it belongs to people rather than to an institution.

### A concrete first slate

If the meeting decides one thing, decide the first ten pages.

For the newly diagnosed:

1. You have just been told you might have Parkinson's. What now.
2. What happens at a neurology appointment, and how to use ten minutes well.
3. What the medicines do, in plain words.
4. Who else should be on your team. Physio, speech therapist, nurse.
5. What to tell family, friends and work, and when.

For carers and family:

6. Someone you love has just been diagnosed. What changes this month.
7. Why he is fine at 11am and stuck at 4pm. "On" and "off" explained.
8. How to help without taking over.

For living with it:

9. Exercise. What actually helps, and how to start on a bad day.
10. Sleep, and why it goes wrong.

Then two printable tools. The appointment questions sheet and the symptom
diary.

Ten pages, well made and properly reviewed, is a real launch. Fifty pages
written by a machine is not.

### What we will not do yet

Saying no here protects the trust we are building.

- No dosage advice and no "ask your doctor to switch you to this".
- No supplements, diets or cures.
- No news or research round ups. They go stale and few people read them.
- No forum or open community. Moderating one properly is a full time job.
- Nothing in an area where we have no reviewer.

## 2. How we verify it

This is the whole product. If a reader can get the same words out of a
chatbot in ten seconds, we have nothing to offer.

### The rule: two signatures on every page

Nothing goes live without both of these.

1. **A clinician signs it off.** Named on the page, with credentials.
2. **A person with lived experience signs it off.** Someone with Parkinson's
   or a carer, named, who confirms it is true to their experience and that
   it can actually be read.

Neither alone is enough. Doctors miss what it is like to live with it.
Patients miss what is clinically wrong. No large site does exactly this, it
is easy to explain, and it belongs on the About page as a promise.

### What the reader sees on every page

This is a build task for the site and it matters as much as the words.

- Written by
- Medically reviewed by, with credentials and institution
- Reviewed for clarity by, and how long they have lived with Parkinson's
- Last reviewed on, and next review due
- Sources at the bottom
- A link to tell us something is wrong

### Where facts come from

No claim without a source, and prefer the settled ones.

Good sources are NICE guidelines, Movement Disorder Society guidance,
Cochrane reviews, and the clinical pages of Parkinson's UK and the
Parkinson's Foundation. Avoid single studies, press releases, news articles,
and anything published by a company selling something. If two good sources
disagree, say so rather than picking one.

### Our position on AI, said publicly

Everyone will assume we use it, so hiding it is the weaker move. Proposed
wording for the site:

> We use AI tools to help draft and edit. No page is published until a named
> doctor and a named person living with Parkinson's have both read it and
> approved it. Their names are on every page.

That turns our biggest credibility risk into an asset. It only works if it
is true every time, including the weeks we are in a hurry.

### Make it easy for a reviewer to say yes

Reviewer time is the bottleneck, not writing. Design the ask to take twenty
minutes.

- Send one page, never a folder.
- Ask four specific questions. Is anything here wrong. Is anything important
  missing. Would you give this to a patient. Is there anything you want
  taken out.
- Give a one week deadline and mean it.
- Credit them by name with a link. Many clinicians will value that.
- Batch the asks. One email a quarter with three pages beats a trickle.

Target five clinical reviewers and five lived experience reviewers. Ideally
a movement disorder neurologist, a Parkinson's nurse, a physiotherapist, a
speech and language therapist, and a psychiatrist or psychologist.

### An advisory board

Ask five respected people to lend their names to a content advisory board.
Low effort for them, high trust for us, and it makes every later reviewer
ask much easier. This is the highest leverage thing anyone can do this
month.

### Corrections

Publish a short corrections policy and honour it. When we get something
wrong we fix it, date it, and say what changed. Nobody expects perfection.
People do notice honesty.

## 3. How we get the word out

Our readers are mostly not on TikTok. Rank channels by how this audience
actually finds things.

### 1. A doctor handing it over

The highest trust route by a distance. A neurologist giving a newly
diagnosed patient a printed sheet with our address on it is worth more than
a thousand impressions. For that to happen the sheet has to save the doctor
time. That is exactly what the appointment questions sheet and the symptom
diary do.

The ask: five doctors we already know. Give each 50 printed copies and ask
for honest feedback in a month. Do not ask them to promote us. Ask them to
use something.

### 2. Support groups and community organisers

Do not drop a link into a group. Go to whoever runs it and offer something
first. A printable, a short talk, help rewriting their handouts. Then ask
whether the group would find our pages useful. WhatsApp groups matter more
than any website in India, and they run on the organiser's say so.

The ask: list the groups we already have a route into, and name who makes
each introduction.

### 3. Search

Answer pages are the right shape for search, and this is the channel that
compounds. Someone searching at 2am on the night of a diagnosis is exactly
our reader. It is slow. Twelve well made pages will do more over two years
than any launch post. It needs each page titled as the question, one
question per page, and a proper sitemap.

### 4. Other organisations

Parkinson's societies, hospital patient education teams, physiotherapy
clinics. Offer our pages for them to use and credit, or offer to translate.
Partnership beats promotion.

### 5. Email

The one channel we own. A short letter once a month with one useful thing in
it. The signup form was removed from the site because it did nothing.
Putting a working one back is a build task, and it should happen before we
tell anyone about the site.

### 6. Social, only where they are

Facebook groups are where older patients and carers actually are. LinkedIn
reaches clinicians, researchers and possible funders. YouTube is worth it
only if we make something demonstrative, like exercise or speech. Skip X and
TikTok for now.

### Launch in two steps

**Quiet launch first.** Send the ten pages to twenty people we know.
Patients, carers, two doctors. Ask one question. Was any of this useful, and
what was missing. Fix what comes back. Two weeks, and it stops us launching
something embarrassing.

**Then the real one.** Only after that, go to the support groups and the
doctors.

### What success looks like in 90 days

Keep the bar honest and small.

- 10 pages live, each with two named reviewers.
- 5 clinical and 5 lived experience reviewers signed up.
- 2 printable tools in use by at least 3 doctors.
- 3 support groups sharing the link because they chose to.
- 100 people on the email list.
- 5 unsolicited messages from strangers saying it helped.

The last one matters most. If we get it, the rest follows. If we do not,
more content will not fix it.

## Decisions for the meeting

Six things only we can decide.

1. **Who is reader number one.** The newly diagnosed person, or the carer.
   Pick one for the first ten pages. My vote is the newly diagnosed person.
   That is the moment of greatest need and the clearest search behaviour.
2. **Where we anchor.** India, or the UK and global, or country neutral with
   local notes added. My vote is India, because the gap and our network are
   both there.
3. **How much we can sustain.** Two pages a month honestly delivered beats
   ten promised. Pick a number we can hold for a year.
4. **Who the first five reviewers are, and who asks them.** Names on paper
   in this meeting.
5. **Whether we say publicly that we use AI to draft.** My vote is yes,
   clearly, on the About page.
6. **What we are not doing.** Agree the no list above, so we can decline
   things later without re-arguing.

## Where Dad can help most

1. Five names for the advisory board, and the introductions.
2. Five clinical reviewers, each asked personally.
3. The first three doctors who will keep a printed sheet on the desk.
4. A read of the first two pages, as a doctor, before anyone else sees them.

## The next four weeks

**Week 1.** Agree the six decisions. Write the first two answer pages. Draft

**Week 2.** Make the reviewer and advisory board asks. Build the review

**Week 3.** Put the first two pages through both reviews. Draft the
